## Where the project stands

SEM2 starts from a user-supplied directed acyclic graph (DAG). A formula declares exogenous variables and their distributions, then defines endogenous variables as functions of their parents. The package parses the formula, constructs the graph, and can generate observational and interventional samples.

Work so far covers three broad settings:

- linear Gaussian and non-Gaussian models;
- nonlinear continuous models, with neural networks for structural functions; and
- finite discrete models, including partial-identification examples.

These parts are not equally mature. Linear models have an exact Gaussian likelihood path, continuous nonlinear models mainly use the energy score, and discrete models currently rely on finite-state probability calculations. Some research implementations still live outside the shared repository.

SEM2 can simulate an intervention by replacing a structural equation with a fixed value and propagating the remaining equations. This gives the intervention implied by the fitted model. It does not, by itself, show that the graph is correct or that the causal effect is identified from the observed data.

## Main discussion

### 1. Separate the model from the data view

The clearest design conclusion was that observed or latent status should not be a permanent property of the structural model.

A variable may be observed in one dataset and missing from another. The formula should therefore describe the system itself: nodes, edges, exogenous distributions, and structural equations. A separate data layer should say:

- which table columns map to which nodes;
- which nodes are observed in each dataset;
- which variables play treatment and outcome roles for a query; and
- whether the data are observational or interventional.

This separation would also allow one model to be fitted to several datasets with different observed margins. Their losses could be combined with explicit weighting for sample size and scale. The framework can support this in principle, but the data abstraction and fitting API do not yet exist.

### 2. Fitting objectives and optimization

The common fitting goal is to make the model-implied distribution on observed variables agree with the empirical distribution. Depending on the model, the criterion may be an exact likelihood or a simulation-based discrepancy.

The meeting discussed the following options:

- **Linear Gaussian likelihood:** already available and preferable when the exact likelihood is tractable.
- **Energy score:** the main general-purpose loss currently used for continuous nonlinear models.
- **MMD:** easy to compute, but its optimization can stall when the generated distribution is already roughly aligned with the data.
- **Sinkhorn divergence:** a possible compromise between MMD and Wasserstein geometry. A contributor reported encouraging preliminary results for heavy-tailed data, but the implementation and experiments were still local and had not been reviewed.
- **Projected or sliced scores:** Sergio suggested these as possible ways to improve high-dimensional behavior by averaging one-dimensional projected scores.

The earlier one-parameter example used a rough Adam loop. The group preferred a reliable optimizer, such as L-BFGS where appropriate, together with convergence diagnostics. Examples should show the full workflow: fit a free parameter, report the result, and use the fitted model for an intervention.

For finite discrete models, exact enumeration is useful for small problems but scales poorly as the state space grows. The discussion also covered continuous relaxations and Gumbel-Softmax, although the reported discrete work used direct probability calculations.

### 3. Identification, bounds, and uncertainty

The group separated two statistical questions:

1. **Identification:** Does the graph and observation regime determine the causal quantity, either as a point or as a set?
2. **Regularity:** Does closeness under the chosen fitting loss imply that the target quantity is also close enough for stable estimation and inference?

A causal quantity may be identifiable but poorly behaved under a particular loss. Conversely, SEM2 may be able to compute a model intervention even when the effect is not identified from the observations.

For the discrete instrumental-variable example, the current strategy is to fit the observed distribution while adding a small positive or negative multiple of the average treatment effect to the objective. Optimizing in both directions searches for compatible models with small and large effects. This is a useful computational approach to partial identification, but one optimization run is not proof of a sharp bound. Restarts, fit tolerances, and stronger numerical or theoretical checks are still needed.

The package currently returns a fitted distribution and point estimates derived from it. It does not yet provide a general confidence-interval method for nonlinear causal functionals. One possible route is semiparametric theory: impose enough smoothness to derive an influence function and asymptotic normality. The group recognized that these assumptions may be strong and difficult to check, especially in the latent-variable problems that motivate SEM2.

A more pragmatic goal is to report the range of causal conclusions across generative models that fit the observations well, while stating the assumptions and avoiding stronger guarantees than the method supports.

### 4. Software priorities

The parser, graph construction, node modules, samplers, and model classes form a useful core. The less developed part is the path from a real table to a robust fit.

The main software gaps discussed were:

- a dataset schema and column-to-node mapping;
- removal of hard-coded treatment and outcome names;
- a clear home and API for reusable loss operators;
- robust trainer wrappers and convergence diagnostics;
- support for multiple observational or experimental datasets; and
- better examples for fitting and intervention.

A long formula may also become awkward for high-dimensional systems. Programmatic graph construction or convenience wrappers for common designs could improve usability later. For now, reliable fitting of a user-supplied graph remains the priority; graph comparison and discovery are future work.

### 5. PFNs as a possible extension

Sergio described PFNs as a form of amortized Bayesian inference. The basic idea is to simulate parameters from a prior, generate datasets from the SCM, and train a transformer to map a variable-size dataset to a posterior distribution. Once trained, the network can process a new dataset in one forward pass rather than running a fresh optimization or MCMC procedure.

The main difficulty is the prior. Priors on coefficients in a linear SCM can be interpretable. Priors on neural-network weights are harder to justify because they may induce unrealistic priors over structural functions. A PFN also learns the posterior under its training simulator; it can perform poorly when the real data differ from that simulated prior.

The discussion distinguished several levels of ambition:

1. fit every dataset and graph separately, as SEM2 does now;
2. train a PFN for repeated datasets under one fixed graph;
3. give the graph as an input to a more general PFN; or
4. ask the PFN to infer the graph.

A fixed-graph PFN is the safest starting point, but it is worthwhile only if its training cost can be reused across many datasets. Passing a user-supplied graph as input better matches SEM2's broader aim, but increases the simulation and generalization challenge. Learning the graph itself was considered too ambitious for the current project because of non-identifiability and strong dependence on graph priors.

A narrower option may be more useful: train the model to predict the posterior distribution of a causal effect or counterfactual outcome directly, rather than the full parameter posterior. This is closer to existing PFN regression tasks and to related workshop work discussed in the meeting.

## Decisions and working direction

- Move observed/latent status out of the structural formula and into a dataset mapping or data-aware operator.
- Keep reliable fitting for a user-supplied graph as the immediate product goal.
- Bring the local Sinkhorn work and remaining discrete implementation into reviewable repository branches before presenting them as package features.
- Replace sampling-only examples with end-to-end fitting and intervention examples.
- Treat identification, regularity under the loss, sampling uncertainty, and optimization error as separate issues.
- Investigate projected or sliced distributional scores.
- Explore PFNs first for a fixed or supplied graph, possibly targeting a causal quantity directly. Leave graph discovery as a separate, lower-priority problem.

## Questions to carry forward

- Which causal quantities are point identified, partially identified, or unidentified for a supplied graph and observed margin?
- Which fitting losses make those quantities stable enough for inference?
- How should the package separate sampling uncertainty, optimization error, and structural non-identification?
- Does Sinkhorn's reported heavy-tail advantage persist in controlled tests?
- What data schema can support different observed margins and a mix of observational and interventional data?
- Can a PFN use a scientifically meaningful prior over nonlinear structural functions, and how robust is it to prior shift?
